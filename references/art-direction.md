# Art Direction

Use this reference when the visual direction is open, a result feels generic, or the task needs typography, color, imagery, shape, or motion decisions.

## Derive a direction; do not select a costume

Build art direction from four inputs:

1. **Task** — what must become obvious or easy?
2. **Audience** — what feels credible, legible, and relevant to them?
3. **Content** — editorial, numeric, social, spatial, transactional, or instructional?
4. **Emotion** — what should the interface reduce, increase, or transform?

A style label is useful only after it produces visible decisions.

| Attribute | Possible visible consequences |
| --- | --- |
| Calm | Stable geometry, restrained chroma, low surface noise, predictable motion |
| Energetic | Strong scale contrast, directional crops, saturated focal color, quick transitions |
| Expert | Dense but ordered information, precise labels, tabular numerals, restrained ornament |
| Human | Warm type or imagery, conversational copy, softer grouping, visible feedback |
| Editorial | Expressive display hierarchy, deliberate measure, image-led rhythm, fewer containers |
| Utilitarian | Compact type scale, direct labels, strong states, minimal decorative layers |

These are starting relationships, not recipes.

### Derivation examples

- A busy gardener needs to find today's unclaimed work; content is a short changing task list and the emotional goal is shared responsibility. A task-first direction could lead with the next action and group work by readiness, using plant imagery only where it helps identify the plot. Risk: decorative photography might push urgent work down.
- A field technician scans a dense schedule between visits; times, locations, and status matter more than mood. A time-led direction could align arrival windows and use compact, explicit status labels, reserving stronger color for exceptions. Risk: density must still survive larger text and long addresses.

These illustrate a chain from task and content to visible decisions, not reusable layouts. Before committing, test the thesis against plausible long content, empty data, large text, and one relevant failure where feasible; adjust if the central idea breaks.

## Direction cards

For open work, produce two or three cards that differ in structure. Keep them short enough to compare.

```text
Name:
Thesis:
Focal point and reading path:
Composition:
Type strategy:
Color/surface behavior:
Imagery or illustration:
Motion character:
Signature moment:
Main risk:
Why it fits:
```

### Structural difference test

Two directions are genuinely different when at least three of these change:

- Focal element.
- Reading path.
- Content grouping.
- Density.
- Type hierarchy.
- Image-to-interface relationship.
- Primary interaction placement.
- Motion model.

Palette swaps, corner-radius changes, and alternative gradients do not create a new direction.

In at least one direction, explore a composition whose primary grouping comes from type, alignment, spacing, dividers, imagery, or data presentation instead of repeated cards. This is a comparison tool, not a requirement that the selected design avoid cards. Preserve existing product constraints when extending a system.

## Selection

Choose one direction with these questions:

- Does it clarify the primary task?
- Does it fit the user and use situation?
- Does its distinctiveness emerge from the product rather than a trend?
- Can it survive real content and relevant states?
- Can it be built credibly with the available system, assets, and platform?

State the rejected direction's main tradeoff. Commit to the selected thesis rather than merging every appealing idea.

## Typography

Use type to create hierarchy before adding containers.

- Choose roles by function: display, title, body, label, annotation, numeric.
- Establish scale contrast intentionally; avoid five nearly identical sizes.
- Use weight, size, width, case, color, and spacing as a coordinated system.
- Keep body measure readable and let text wrap naturally.
- Use tabular numerals where changing numeric values must align.
- Test the longest plausible text and platform text scaling.
- Treat custom fonts as dependencies with licensing, language coverage, and fallback needs.

Common failure: a large heading followed by uniformly gray text and identical rounded cards. The heading is not enough if the rest of the hierarchy is flat.

## Color and surfaces

Assign colors by role before choosing individual values:

- Canvas and elevated surfaces.
- Primary and secondary content.
- Action and focus.
- Informational, success, warning, and destructive states.
- Borders, separators, scrims, and disabled states.

Reserve the strongest contrast or chroma for the intended focal behavior. Do not use accent color everywhere and then expect it to identify the primary action.

Depth may come from contrast, overlap, border, blur, material, or shadow. Do not stack all of them by default.

## Shape and containers

- Group by relationship, not by the desire to place everything in a card.
- Let alignment and spacing do work before adding a surface.
- Use radius families deliberately; excessive pill shapes weaken structure.
- Use border and elevation to express hierarchy or interaction, not decoration alone.
- Break the grid only to create a controlled focal moment.

## Imagery and illustration

Define the image's job:

- Explain a concept.
- Establish identity or place.
- Provide evidence.
- Carry emotional tone.
- Show user-created content.

Specify crop behavior, aspect ratio, safe focal region, loading/failure behavior, and when the image reduces or disappears. Decorative art must not push the task below the fold on constrained screens.

Do not imitate a reference product's proprietary illustration language. Extract the principle and transform it for this context.

## Signature moment

One memorable moment is often stronger than decoration distributed everywhere. It can be:

- A distinctive content transition.
- A typography-led state change.
- A useful visualization.
- A spatial interaction tied to the task.
- An image treatment that makes product content recognizable.

The moment must reinforce comprehension, confidence, delight, or product identity. Remove it if the interface works better without it.

## Anti-template diagnosis

Ask:

- Could the product name be swapped without changing the screen?
- Is the hierarchy derived from the content, or from a familiar template?
- Is every region a rounded card because relationships were not resolved?
- Are gradient, glass, glow, or illustration effects compensating for weak composition?
- Does the type system express the product, or merely use a default sans font at several sizes?
- Is there one coherent visual thesis?
- What visible decisions follow from this product's content or interactions? For substantial work, identify up to three concrete examples when available; do not fabricate distinctions.
- Is iconography coherent across native symbols, custom icons, imagery, and emoji? Does any mixture have an intentional purpose?

Fix the underlying composition or premise instead of layering more styling on top.
